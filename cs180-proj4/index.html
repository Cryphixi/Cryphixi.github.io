Two photos taken from the same center of projection sit on the top rung: a projective transform, or homography. I click matching points in each pair and solve for the 8 unknown entries of H.

Why more than 4 points

  • H has 8 unknowns (h₉ fixed to 1). Each point pair gives 2 equations, one for x and one for y, so 4 pairs is the bare minimum for an exact solution.
  • Exactly 4 pairs fits those clicks perfectly, including their click errors, so one sloppy click warps the whole result.
  • With 8 to 10 spread-out pairs the system is over-determined; least squares finds the h that best fits all of them, averaging out click noise, like fitting a line to 10 noisy points instead of 2.

Reprojection error: about 8 px mean, 13 px max. That remainder is expected: the facade isn’t perfectly flat, since the columns and entrance surround stick out from the wall. computeH also passes synthetic tests for identity, translation, scale, and a known projective H.

What went wrong while clicking
  1. Points in one vertical line. H came out with diagonal ~0.7 and the wrong shift: least squares can’t separate scale, shift and perspective when every point shares the same x. Fix: spread points across top, middle and bottom, left and right of the overlap.
  2. One pair clicked in the wrong order. Clicking im2 then im1 flipped H (negative diagonal). Caught it because its shift was +294 px while every other pair was about −300 px.
  3. A pair on a smooth column. No corner, so I clicked the column at different heights in each image. That single pair raised the mean error from ~10 px to ~22 px. Removed it, plus a point on the recessed door glass, which is off the facade plane and has parallax.

Least squares can’t ignore bad clicks: check each pair’s shift for consistency, and only click sharp corners on the same plane.

# computeH: least-squares homography from n ≥ 4 pairs
def computeH(im1_pts, im2_pts):
    A, b = [], []
    for (x, y), (xp, yp) in zip(im1_pts, im2_pts):
        A.append([x, y, 1, 0, 0, 0, -x*xp, -y*xp]); b.append(xp)
        A.append([0, 0, 0, x, y, 1, -x*yp, -y*yp]); b.append(yp)
    h, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(np.array(A), np.array(b), rcond=None)
    return np.append(h, 1).reshape(3, 3)

Warp the Images

Every output pixel asks where it came from.

Inverse warping walks the output canvas, sends each pixel back through , and samples the source there. Nothing is pushed forward, so there are no holes.

  • Bounding box. A homography maps straight lines to straight lines, so the warped image is still four-sided. I push the corners (0,0), (w−1,0), (0,h−1), (w−1,h−1) through H with apply_H and take the min/max, returned as [[xmin, ymin], [xmax, ymax]]. The mins can be negative (doe_1 lands at xmin ≈ −377), which is why the warp also returns an offset.
  • Nearest neighbor. Round the source coordinate and read one pixel.
  • Bilinear. Floor (not round) to the top-left neighbor , take , and blend 4 pixels with weights , which sum to 1. The valid mask is tighter than NN () because the +1 neighbors must also be inside the image.

Rectification

rectify(im, pts, target_pts, warp_fn) is computeH then warp_fn, so the same function runs both NN and bilinear. I click 4 corners of a real-world rectangle in order TL, TR, BR, BL, and the target is an upright rectangle anchored at the first click, with separate side_w and side_h so non-square shapes like windows aren’t squashed.

Speed vs quality

On 1000 px wide images, bilinear is about 2× slower than NN because it reads and blends 4 source pixels per output pixel instead of 1. The apartment takes longer for both because its warped canvas is larger. Overall the two look nearly identical (mean color difference ~3/255 on the doe_1 test warp), but zoomed in, NN shows staircase jaggies on tilted edges: window mullions, skylight grid lines, railings. Bilinear edges are smooth.

What went wrong: Doe Library (doe_0)

  1. Clicked the photo-frame corners, starting bottom-left. The output squished into a square and flipped upside down; clicked order must match target order.
  2. One small window pane (~30 × 65 px), square target. Extreme smearing: tiny click errors got magnified, and the tall pane was forced into a square.
  3. Added side_w / side_h, still a small region (~50 × 75 px). Still a huge stretch.
  4. Root cause: a very steep viewing angle. Straightening that plane pushes the sky near its vanishing line toward infinity, so the canvas becomes enormous, mostly stretched sky, with the building in a corner. Real projective geometry, not a code bug.
  5. Fix: switched to apartment_3, a moderate-angle facade with a large clear rectangle.

Click a large region, in the same order as the target, match the target’s aspect ratio to the real object, and avoid surfaces viewed at extreme angles.

(xmin, ymin), (xmax, ymax) = compute_output_bounds(im, H)
xs, ys = np.meshgrid(np.arange(xmin, xmax+1), np.arange(ymin, ymax+1))
src = apply_H(np.linalg.inv(H), np.c_[xs.ravel(), ys.ravel()])
x, y = src[:, 0], src[:, 1]
x0, y0 = np.floor(x).astype(int), np.floor(y).astype(int)
valid = (x0 >= 0) & (y0 >= 0) & (x0 < w-1) & (y0 < h-1)
dx, dy = (x - x0)[valid, None], (y - y0)[valid, None]
a, b = im[y0[valid], x0[valid]], im[y0[valid], x0[valid]+1]
c, d = im[y0[valid]+1, x0[valid]], im[y0[valid]+1, x0[valid]+1]
out[valid] = (1-dx)*(1-dy)*a + dx*(1-dy)*b + (1-dx)*dy*c + dx*dy*d
A.4

Blend into a Mosaic

  • Reference frame. The middle photo is the reference and is not warped (H = identity). The left and right photos each get their own H straight into its frame, so no chaining was needed.
  • Shared canvas. build_mosaic warps all three with my bilinear warp, takes the union of their bounding boxes, and pastes each into its own full-canvas layer at (xmin − min_x, ymin − min_y).
  • Blending. Plain overlaying leaves hard edges and brightness steps. I weight each layer by a distance transform of its alpha mask: 0 at the image edge, growing toward its center. Each photo dominates near its own center and fades toward its edge, so the transition is smooth instead of a seam.

What went wrong

In two point files, one pair was clicked right-image-first, which flipped H and wrecked the mosaic (mean errors of 312 and 804 px). Caught by checking that every pair shifts the same direction; swapping those two pairs back brought errors to ~5 to 8 px.

Artifacts that remain
  • People moved between shots, so they appear ghosted in the overlap.
  • The Doe doorway is recessed, off the wall’s plane, so it doubles slightly.
  • Distant Glade treetops and hills double slightly because my points sat mostly on nearer features; mixing near and far points helps.
